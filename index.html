How to Explain a Technical Product to Non-Technical Buyers

By Umar · 5 min read

Most technical products don't lose deals because they are bad. They lose because the person holding the budget never understood what they were buying.

The CTO read the documentation and loved it. The CFO read the landing page and felt nothing. In the end, the CFO signs the contract, or doesn't. If you create content for a technology company, your real job is to close that gap. Here are five rules that help.

1. Start with the problem, not the product

Buyers don't wake up wanting an "AI-powered platform". They wake up with a late project, a rising cloud bill or a team that keeps missing deadlines. Open with that problem in the exact words your audience would use in a meeting.

If your first paragraph could be copied onto a competitor's website, it isn't saying anything yet.

2. Replace jargon with a picture

Every technical term is a small wall. Some readers will climb it; many will simply leave. Swap abstractions for something concrete. An API is a waiter carrying orders between the kitchen and the table. A load balancer is the host who decides which table gets the next guest.

Quick testRead a sentence out loud to someone outside your industry. If they ask "what does that mean?", rewrite it.

3. Talk about outcomes in numbers

"Faster deployments" is a feature. "Releases that took two days now take two hours" is a reason to buy. Whenever you have real figures from a customer or a pilot, put them near the top. When you don't have them yet, say what you measure and how, instead of inventing impressive-sounding claims.

4. Write for two readers at once

Technical content on LinkedIn is read by engineers and by the people who approve their spending. Give the engineer enough detail to trust you, and give the executive a short summary that stands alone. A clear opening, a simple structure and a closing takeaway usually does both.

5. End with one clear next step

A great article that ends with "contact us for more" wastes the reader's momentum. Offer one specific action: a short demo, a checklist, a 15-minute call. Make it easy to say yes and obvious what happens afterwards.

Measure, then improve

Publishing is the start. Track impressions, reading depth, comments and the number of profile visits or enquiries after each article. Compare topics, not just numbers: which problem made people stop scrolling? Do more of that, and cut what nobody finished.

The short version

Lead with the problem, use pictures instead of jargon, prove it with numbers, write for both readers, and give one next step. Technical buyers are people first, and people buy what they understand.
